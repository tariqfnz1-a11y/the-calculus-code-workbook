Problem P1 of 10 ⬜

Question: Why is calculus called "arithmetic for things that move"? Explain using the concepts of derivative and integral.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Think about the speedometer and odometer. How do they relate to arithmetic operations?

Calculus is called "arithmetic for things that move" because it extends arithmetic to describe motion and change.

  • The derivative (like a speedometer) tells you how fast something is changing right now — it's the arithmetic of instantaneous change.
  • The integral (like an odometer) tells you the total accumulation — it's the arithmetic of adding up tiny changes.

Just as arithmetic uses addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, calculus uses these operations to describe change, motion, and accumulation.

Problem P2 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain the 0/0 paradox and how the limit trick solves it.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Speed = Distance ÷ Time. What are the values at an instant? How do we get around dividing by zero?

The 0/0 paradox occurs when calculating instant speed: at an instant, distance = 0 and time = 0, so speed = 0 ÷ 0, which is undefined.

The limit trick solves this by zooming in on smaller and smaller time intervals. Instead of dividing 0 by 0, we look at what the average speed approaches as the interval shrinks to zero.

This number — the limit — is the instant speed. We never divide by zero; we just look at the number the average speed is approaching.

Problem P3 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain why slope and speed are the same concept. Use the ideas of rise over run and the Mental Zoom.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Both are "one thing divided by another." What do you divide in each case? How does the Mental Zoom connect them?

Slope = Rise ÷ Run = change in y ÷ change in x.

Speed = Distance ÷ Time = change in distance ÷ change in time.

Both are one change divided by another — they measure the rate of change.

The Mental Zoom connects them: when you zoom in on a distance-time graph, the curve becomes a straight line. The slope of that line is the speed. So speed is just the slope of a distance-time graph!

Problem P4 of 10 ⬜

Question: In your own words, explain the Power Rule. Why is it a "shortcut," and how does it relate to the zoom-in method?

(Write a complete explanation.)

What does the Power Rule do? How long does it take compared to the zoom-in method?

The Power Rule is a shortcut for finding derivatives: for $f(x) = x^n$, $f'(x) = n \times x^{n-1}$.

It's a "shortcut" because it replaces the slow, labor-intensive zoom-in method (calculating slopes over shrinking intervals) with one step of multiplication and subtraction.

The Power Rule gives the same result as the zoom-in method — it's just faster. It's a pattern that mathematicians discovered by seeing the pattern in the zoom-in method.

Problem P5 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain the relationship between the derivative and the integral. Use the analogy of a speedometer and odometer.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Think about the speedometer and odometer in a car. What does each do? How are they connected?

The derivative and integral are opposites — they undo each other.

  • The derivative is like a speedometer — it tells you how fast something is changing right now.
  • The integral is like an odometer — it tells you the total accumulation over time.

If you know the distance, the derivative gives you speed. If you know speed, the integral gives you distance. They are inverse operations, just like addition and subtraction.

Problem P6 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain why $e^x$ is called the "super-function." What makes it special compared to other functions?

(Write a complete explanation.)

What is the derivative of $e^x$? What is the integral of $e^x$? No other function has this property.

$e^x$ is called the super-function because it is its own derivative AND its own integral.

  • $\frac{d}{dx} e^x = e^x$
  • $\int e^x \, dx = e^x + C$

No other function has this property. It means $e^x$ never changes when you differentiate or integrate — it's the ultimate "self-similar" function.

It also describes natural growth — where the rate of growth is proportional to the current amount.

Problem P7 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain the derivative cycle for sine and cosine. Why does it take four steps to return to the start?

(Write a complete explanation.)

What is the derivative of $\sin(x)$? What is the derivative of $\cos(x)$? Keep going until you return to $\sin(x)$.

The derivative cycle for sine and cosine is:

$\sin(x) \to \cos(x) \to -\sin(x) \to -\cos(x) \to \sin(x)$

It takes four steps to return to the start because each derivative changes the function, and it takes four changes to get back to the original.

  • Step 1: $\sin \to \cos$
  • Step 2: $\cos \to -\sin$
  • Step 3: $-\sin \to -\cos$
  • Step 4: $-\cos \to \sin$

This cycle is the foundation of wave motion and alternating current.

Problem P8 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain why the derivative is zero at both maximums and minimums. Use the hill and valley analogy.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Think about walking up a hill and then down. What happens to the slope at the very top?

The derivative is zero at maximums and minimums because the curve is flat at those points.

  • At a maximum (top of a hill): the slope changes from positive (going up) to negative (going down). At the peak, the slope is zero.
  • At a minimum (bottom of a valley): the slope changes from negative (going down) to positive (going up). At the bottom, the slope is zero.

This is why optimization works: find where the derivative is zero, and you've found a maximum or minimum.

Problem P9 of 10 ⬜

Question: Explain how the Mental Zoom connects slope, speed, area, and accumulation.

(Write a complete explanation.)

What happens when you zoom in on a curve? What happens when you zoom out and add more rectangles?

The Mental Zoom connects these ideas by showing how zooming in and zooming out reveal different aspects of calculus:

  • Slope: Zoom in on a curve → it becomes a straight line → the slope is the derivative.
  • Speed: Zoom in on a distance-time curve → the slope is the instant speed.
  • Area: Zoom out and add more rectangles → the rectangles become the curve → the area is the integral.
  • Accumulation: Zoom out and add up all the tiny slices → the total is the accumulation.

The Mental Zoom is the unifying visual of calculus!

Problem P10 of 10 ⬜

Question: Why is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus called the "Great Reversal"? Explain its importance in your own words.

(Write a complete explanation.)

Before the theorem, slope (derivative) and area (integral) were seen as separate. What did the theorem reveal?

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is called the "Great Reversal" because it reversed how people thought about calculus.

Before the theorem, slope (derivative) and area (integral) were seen as separate ideas. The theorem revealed that they are opposites — they undo each other.

  • If you differentiate and then integrate, you get back where you started.
  • If you integrate and then differentiate, you get back where you started.

This unified two separate branches of calculus into one elegant theory — it's the single most important idea in calculus.
