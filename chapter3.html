Chapter 3: The Instant Speedometer

"Solving the 0/0 Paradox — Finding speed at a frozen moment."

Problem 1 of 10 ⬜

Question: What is the "0/0 problem" when calculating instant speed?

(Hint: At an instant, what are the values of distance and time?)

Speed = Distance ÷ Time. At a single instant, what is the distance traveled? What is the time taken?

At an exact instant, the distance traveled is 0 and the time taken is 0.

So instant speed = $0 \div 0$, which is undefined — it doesn't make sense mathematically.

This is the 0/0 paradox that calculus was invented to solve.

Problem 2 of 10 ⬜

Question: If a car travels 100 meters in 5 seconds, what is its average speed?

Average speed = Total distance ÷ Total time.

Average speed = 100 meters ÷ 5 seconds = 20 m/s

This is the average speed over the whole 5-second interval.

The instant speed at any single moment could be different.

Problem 3 of 10 ⬜

Question: What is the limit trick in calculus used for?

When you can't calculate something directly (like $0 \div 0$), you look at smaller and smaller intervals and see what number the average is approaching.

The limit trick is used to find the instant speed by looking at smaller and smaller time intervals.

As the intervals get tiny, the average speed gets closer and closer to a specific number — that's the instant speed.

We never divide by zero. We just look at what the speed is approaching.

Problem 4 of 10 ⬜

Question: When you zoom in on a curve, what does it look like?

Think about the Mental Zoom from Chapter 1. What happens when you zoom in far enough?

When you zoom in far enough on any smooth curve, it looks like a straight line.

The slope of that straight line is the derivative (the instant speed).

This is the Mental Zoom principle.

Problem 5 of 10 ⬜

Question: For the function $d = t^2$, what is the derivative (the instant speed formula)?

(Hint: From the chapter, the pattern is: speed = 2 × t)

In Chapter 3, we found that for $d = t^2$, the instant speed is $2t$.

For $d = t^2$, the derivative (instant speed) is:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}(t^2) = 2t $$

This means: at any time $t$, the instant speed is $2 \times t$.

At $t = 2$, speed = 4 m/s. At $t = 3$, speed = 6 m/s.

Problem 6 of 10 ⬜

Question: For $d = t^2$, what is the instant speed at $t = 3$ seconds?

(Hint: Use the pattern: speed = $2t$)

Speed = $2 \times t$. Plug in $t = 3$.

Speed = $2 \times t$

At $t = 3$: Speed = $2 \times 3 = 6 m/s$

At exactly 3 seconds, the car is going 6 m/s.

Problem 7 of 10 ⬜

Question: Why can't we just calculate instant speed directly by dividing distance by time?

Speed = Distance ÷ Time. What happens at an instant?

At an exact instant, the distance is 0 and the time is 0.

So instant speed = $0 \div 0$, which is undefined.

This is why we use the limit trick instead — we look at what the average speed approaches as the time interval shrinks to zero.

Problem 8 of 10 ⬜

Question: What is the formula for average speed?

(Hint: It's the total change in distance divided by the total change in time.)

Average speed = (change in distance) ÷ (change in time). In symbols: $\Delta d \div \Delta t$.

Average speed = Distance traveled ÷ Time taken

In symbols: Average speed = $\Delta d \div \Delta t$

Where $\Delta d$ is the change in distance and $\Delta t$ is the change in time.

This gives the average speed over an interval.

Problem 9 of 10 ⬜

Question: What does the derivative measure in calculus?

Think about the speedometer analogy. What does the derivative tell you?

The derivative measures the instant speed — the rate of change at a single moment.

It's the slope of the curve after zooming in infinitely.

In other words: "How fast is it changing right now?"
